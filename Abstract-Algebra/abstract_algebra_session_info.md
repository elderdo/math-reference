# Abstract Algebra & Mathematical Proofs Study Resources

This document summarizes the curated YouTube courses, playlists, and foundational paths discussed during our session to help you transition from mathematical logic to proof-based Abstract Algebra.

## 1. Symbolic Logic & Mathematical Notation
Before diving into proofs, these courses focus on teaching you the foundational language, quantifiers ($\\forall, \\exists$), and operators ($\\implies, \\iff$) of higher mathematics.

* **Trefor Bazett – Discrete Math I**: Focuses on propositional logic, truth tables, and translating English statements into symbolic math notation.
* **The Bright Side of Mathematics – Set Theory & Mathematical Logic**: A rigorous, visually organized German-engineered course covering logical symbols and core set theory notation.
* **Wrath of Math – Mathematical Logic**: Short, bite-sized videos explaining individual connective symbols, logical rules, and inference.
* **Kimberly Brehm – Discrete Mathematics I**: A structured classroom series with its entire first section dedicated to symbolic logic and parsing conditional statements.

## 2. Transition to Proof-Writing
These resources serve as the bridging step, teaching you the formal frameworks and strategies required to construct and format your own mathematical proofs.

* **Trefor Bazett – Introduction to Mathematical Proofs**: A beginner-friendly breakdown of the four core proof methods: Direct, Contrapositive, Contradiction, and Induction.
* **Michael Penn – Introduction to Proofs**: A highly rigorous, academic series applying foundational logic and set operations to standard number theory examples.
* **The Math Sorcerer – Mathematical Proofs**: A vast library of concrete, worked-out proof examples ideal for real-time practice and reference.
* **Socratica – Abstract Algebra Prerequisites**: A highly polished, conceptual series targeting the exact logic structures needed prior to abstract algebra.

### Core Proof Strategies Covered
1. **Direct Proof**: Assume $P$ is true, logically deduce that $Q$ must be true ($P \\implies Q$).
2. **Contrapositive**: Prove that the absence of the conclusion implies the absence of the hypothesis ($\\neg Q \\implies \\neg P$).
3. **Contradiction**: Assume the conclusion is false and show that it breaks an established mathematical law.
4. **Induction**: Establish a base case for $n=1$, assume it holds for $n=k$, and prove it must logically hold for $n=k+1$.

## 3. Abstract Algebra Courses (Rings & Groups)
Once notation and proof strategies are comfortable, these comprehensive courses cover undergraduate abstract algebra topics including groups, rings, fields, and homomorphisms.

* **Math With Richard – Abstract Algebra I**: An undergraduate lecture playlist with dedicated segments on ring definitions, subrings, ring homomorphisms, and the first isomorphism theorems.
* **Bill Kinney – Abstract (Modern) Algebra Course Lectures**: A complete university-level sequence tracking foundational groups through advanced ring theory and fields.
* **Michael Penn – Abstract Algebra: The Basics of Rings**: Focuses deeply on ring theory with concrete examples and rigorous, step-by-step proofs.
